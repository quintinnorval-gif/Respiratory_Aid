Part 1: Anatomy of the Respiratory System

Focus: structure, organisation, and the relationship between anatomy and gas exchange.

Learning Outcomes

  • Identify structures of the upper and lower respiratory tract.
  • Describe the organisation of the respiratory system, including conducting and respiratory zones.
  • Explain the structure of alveoli and the respiratory membrane.
  • Classify respiratory structures according to function.
  • Differentiate between the right and left lungs in structure and function.
  • Relate alveolar structure to efficient gas exchange.

Learning Outcome 1 Answer

The respiratory system can be divided into an upper and a lower respiratory tract. The upper respiratory tract includes the nose, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and pharynx. These structures mainly prepare the air by filtering, warming, and humidifying it before it travels deeper into the body. The lower respiratory tract includes the larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and lungs. These structures conduct air toward the gas exchange surfaces of the lungs, and the alveoli within the lungs are the main site where oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged.

Helpful video: Respiratory anatomy overview

Learning Outcome 2 Answer

The respiratory system is organised into a conducting zone and a respiratory zone. The conducting zone extends from the nose to the terminal bronchioles and is responsible for moving air and conditioning it. No gas exchange occurs here. The respiratory zone includes the respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and alveoli. This is the part of the system where oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out. This organisation is clinically important because some diseases mainly affect airflow, while others mainly affect diffusion and gas exchange.

Helpful video: Conducting and respiratory zones

Learning Outcome 3 Answer

Alveoli are microscopic air sacs with extremely thin walls. They are lined mainly by simple squamous epithelial cells, which reduce the distance for diffusion. Each alveolus is closely surrounded by capillaries, bringing blood very close to the inspired air. The respiratory membrane is made up of the alveolar epithelium, the capillary endothelium, and the fused membranes between them. This arrangement allows oxygen to pass rapidly into the blood and carbon dioxide to leave the blood efficiently.

Helpful video: Alveoli and gas exchange

Learning Outcome 4 Answer

Respiratory structures can be classified according to whether they mainly conduct air or mainly participate in gas exchange. The nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, and most bronchioles are air-conducting structures. Their job is to move air into the lungs and condition it. In contrast, the respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and alveoli are gas exchange structures. These are the parts where oxygen enters the bloodstream and carbon dioxide is removed.

Learning Outcome 5 Answer

The right and left lungs are not identical. The right lung has three lobes and is generally wider and slightly shorter, partly because of the position of the liver. The left lung has two lobes and includes the cardiac notch, which provides space for the heart. These differences are important when interpreting radiographs and when describing the location of disease.

Radiograph resource: Normal chest radiograph anatomy

Learning Outcome 6 Answer

Alveoli are ideal for gas exchange because they have three major structural advantages. First, they have very thin walls, which reduce diffusion distance. Second, they provide a very large total surface area, allowing a large amount of gas exchange to occur at once. Third, they are surrounded by a dense capillary network, which keeps blood flowing past them and maintains the gradients needed for diffusion. In simple terms, the alveoli are built to make gas exchange fast and efficient.

Core Explanation

The respiratory system is divided into an upper and a lower respiratory tract. The upper respiratory tract includes the nose, nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and pharynx. These structures prepare the air before it reaches the lungs by warming it, filtering it, and humidifying it.

The lower respiratory tract includes the larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and lungs. These structures mainly conduct air into the lungs and, at the level of the alveoli, allow gas exchange to occur.

Conducting vs Respiratory Zones

The conducting zone extends from the nose to the terminal bronchioles. Its function is to move and prepare air, but no gas exchange takes place here.

The respiratory zone includes the respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, and alveoli. This is the part of the system where oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out of the blood.

The conducting zone moves air from the outside environment toward the lungs and prepares it by filtering, warming, and humidifying it. No gas exchange takes place there. The respiratory zone is where gas exchange occurs, because oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out. This answer is important because it helps students distinguish between airflow problems and diffusion problems.

Part 2: Physiology of Breathing and Gas Exchange

Focus: ventilation, lung volumes, gas transport, and interpretation of normal values.

Learning Outcomes

  • Define key respiratory terms, including tidal volume, vital capacity, and lung capacity.
  • State normal respiratory values, including respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and arterial blood gases.
  • Explain pulmonary ventilation and distinguish it from external and internal respiration.
  • Differentiate between external respiration, internal respiration, and cellular respiration.
  • Describe the mechanisms of oxygen and carbon dioxide transport in the blood.
  • Interpret normal and abnormal respiratory rates and basic blood gas values.
  • Apply knowledge of lung volumes to clinical scenarios.
  • Differentiate between oxygen transport and carbon dioxide transport.
  • Examine factors affecting gas exchange.
  • Predict the effects of impaired ventilation or gas exchange on oxygenation and carbon dioxide levels.

Tidal volume is the amount of air moved in a normal quiet breath, usually around 500 mL in an adult. Vital capacity is the maximum amount of air that can be exhaled after a full inspiration. Total lung capacity is the total amount of air the lungs can hold. These terms help describe lung function in measurable ways.

Normal respiratory values provide an essential baseline. A normal adult respiratory rate is 12 to 20 breaths per minute. Oxygen saturation is usually 95 to 100%. PaO₂ is about 80 to 100 mmHg, PaCO₂ is 35 to 45 mmHg, and pH is 7.35 to 7.45. These values help identify whether a patient is oxygenating and ventilating adequately.

Pulmonary ventilation refers to the movement of air into and out of the lungs. External respiration refers to gas exchange between alveoli and blood, while internal respiration refers to gas exchange between blood and tissues. Students should understand that ventilation is movement, while respiration is exchange.

External respiration occurs in the lungs, internal respiration occurs between blood and tissues, and cellular respiration occurs inside cells where oxygen is used to make ATP. This distinction is important because breathing problems can occur at different levels and not all oxygen problems are caused by poor ventilation.

Oxygen is transported mainly bound to haemoglobin, forming oxyhaemoglobin. Carbon dioxide is transported mainly as bicarbonate ions, with smaller amounts bound to haemoglobin or dissolved in plasma. This means oxygen transport depends heavily on haemoglobin, while carbon dioxide transport is closely related to acid–base balance.

Helpful video: Oxygen and carbon dioxide transport

Normal values suggest stable breathing and gas exchange. Abnormal respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, or blood gas values suggest respiratory compromise. For example, a high respiratory rate can indicate distress, a low PaO₂ suggests poor oxygenation, and a raised PaCO₂ suggests inadequate ventilation.

Lung volumes can help explain clinical patterns. In shallow breathing, tidal volume decreases, so less fresh air reaches the alveoli. In deep breathing, tidal volume increases, which often improves alveolar ventilation. This makes lung volumes useful when interpreting fatigue, pain, or respiratory distress.

Helpful video: Lung volumes and spirometry

Oxygen transport mainly depends on haemoglobin binding and delivery to tissues. Carbon dioxide transport mainly depends on the bicarbonate system and contributes strongly to pH control. This difference helps explain why anaemia affects oxygen delivery, while hypoventilation strongly affects carbon dioxide and acid–base status.

Gas exchange depends on a large surface area, a thin membrane, and good blood flow. If the surface area decreases, as in emphysema, oxygen uptake is reduced. If the membrane becomes thicker, as in pneumonia, diffusion is slower. If blood flow is reduced, as in pulmonary embolism, gas exchange becomes ineffective even if ventilation is present.

If ventilation is impaired, carbon dioxide is retained and PaCO₂ rises. If gas exchange is impaired, oxygenation falls and PaO₂ drops. Some patients may develop both problems at the same time. Students should be able to predict whether the main problem is oxygen failure, carbon dioxide failure, or both.

Ventilation and Respiration

Pulmonary ventilation is the physical movement of air into and out of the lungs. This includes inhalation and exhalation. External respiration refers to gas exchange between the alveoli and the blood. Internal respiration refers to gas exchange between the blood and the tissues. Cellular respiration is different from both of these, because it refers to the use of oxygen by cells to produce energy in the form of ATP.

Lung Volumes and Normal Values

Tidal volume is the amount of air moved during a normal quiet breath and is about 500 mL in adults. Vital capacity is the maximum amount of air exhaled after a deep inhalation. Total lung capacity is the total volume of air the lungs can hold. A normal adult respiratory rate is 12 to 20 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation is generally 95 to 100%, PaO₂ is about 80 to 100 mmHg, PaCO₂ is 35 to 45 mmHg, and pH is 7.35 to 7.45.

Internal respiration is the exchange of gases between blood and tissues. Cellular respiration is the use of oxygen by cells to produce energy. This answer matters because students often use these terms as if they mean the same thing, but they describe different levels of respiratory function.

Tidal volume is the volume of air moved during a normal breath. Vital capacity is the maximum amount of air exhaled after the deepest possible inhalation. Total lung capacity is the full amount of air the lungs can hold after maximum inspiration. These values are useful for understanding normal lung function and disease patterns.

In shallow breathing, tidal volume becomes smaller. Even if the patient breathes more quickly, less fresh air reaches the alveoli with each breath. This means alveolar ventilation can still be poor, and gas exchange may remain inadequate. In simple terms, the patient is breathing fast but not effectively.

Part 3: Regulation of Breathing

Focus: respiratory centres, chemoreceptors, acid–base balance, and clinical signs of distress.

Learning Outcomes

  • Identify respiratory centres in the medulla oblongata and pons.
  • Identify central and peripheral chemoreceptors and their locations.
  • Explain the role of chemoreceptors in regulating breathing in response to CO₂, O₂, and pH changes.
  • Describe normal and abnormal breathing patterns.
  • Apply knowledge of chemoreceptor responses to explain hypercapnia and hypoxia.
  • Interpret clinical signs such as cyanosis, altered breathing rate, and oxygen saturation.
  • Analyse the relationship between CO₂ levels, pH, and respiratory compensation.
  • Evaluate respiratory conditions using physiological principles.
  • Justify appropriate interventions based on patient presentation.

The respiratory centres are located in the medulla oblongata and the pons. The medulla generates the basic rhythm of breathing, while the pons helps smooth and adjust that rhythm. Together, they control automatic breathing without conscious effort.

Central chemoreceptors are located in the medulla and respond mainly to carbon dioxide and pH changes. Peripheral chemoreceptors are found in the carotid and aortic bodies and respond mainly to low oxygen levels. Knowing their locations helps explain different breathing responses in health and disease.

Chemoreceptors help regulate breathing by constantly monitoring blood chemistry. When carbon dioxide rises or pH falls, the respiratory centres are stimulated to increase breathing. When oxygen levels fall significantly, peripheral chemoreceptors help increase respiratory drive. This is one of the body’s key homeostatic mechanisms.

Helpful video: Chemoreceptors and breathing regulation

Normal breathing is called eupnoea. Tachypnoea is rapid breathing, bradypnoea is slow breathing, and apnoea is absent breathing. Hyperventilation refers to excessive breathing that lowers carbon dioxide, while Kussmaul breathing is a deep pattern seen in severe acidosis. These patterns often provide important clinical clues.

Hypercapnia means carbon dioxide is elevated, and it strongly stimulates breathing because it lowers pH. Hypoxia means oxygen levels are low, and it stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors. Students should understand that carbon dioxide is usually the main driver of breathing in healthy people, while oxygen becomes particularly important in severe disease or chronic hypercapnic states.

Cyanosis suggests low oxygenation. An altered respiratory rate may suggest distress, fatigue, or compensation. Low oxygen saturation confirms that oxygenation is reduced. Clinical signs should always be interpreted together, not in isolation, because one sign alone may not show the whole picture.

Carbon dioxide and pH are closely linked. When carbon dioxide rises, more acid is formed and pH falls, causing respiratory acidosis. When carbon dioxide falls, acidity decreases and pH rises, causing respiratory alkalosis. This is why ventilation has such a strong effect on acid–base balance.

Helpful video: Respiratory acidosis and alkalosis

Respiratory conditions can be evaluated by identifying the main physiological problem. Asthma is mainly an airflow problem, pneumonia is mainly a gas exchange problem, COPD includes airflow limitation and carbon dioxide retention, and pulmonary embolism is mainly a perfusion problem. This approach helps students integrate anatomy, physiology, and clinical reasoning.

Appropriate interventions depend on the physiological problem. Oxygen is given to correct hypoxia, ventilation support is used to improve carbon dioxide removal, and upright positioning helps improve lung expansion. A strong answer should justify the intervention based on what is going wrong in the patient’s respiratory system.

The respiratory centres are found in the medulla oblongata and pons. The medulla sets the basic breathing rhythm, while the pons refines and modifies that rhythm. Together they ensure that breathing continues automatically and can adapt to the body’s needs.

Central chemoreceptors are located in the medulla and respond mainly to carbon dioxide and pH. Peripheral chemoreceptors are located in the carotid and aortic bodies and respond mainly to low oxygen levels. A complete answer should mention both the location and the main stimulus.

When carbon dioxide levels increase, the blood becomes more acidic. Chemoreceptors detect this change and stimulate the respiratory centres to increase the rate and depth of breathing. This helps remove excess carbon dioxide and restore acid–base balance. In simple terms, high CO₂ tells the body it needs to breathe more.

Helpful video: Why high CO₂ increases breathing

Respiratory acidosis develops when ventilation is inadequate because carbon dioxide is not removed effectively. Carbon dioxide then accumulates in the blood and contributes to acid formation, which lowers pH. This is why hypoventilation is strongly associated with respiratory acidosis.

Integrated Case Study Series

Each case follows the clinical flow of EMS first response, MITS diagnostic confirmation, and Nursing care or aftercare.

Case 1: Asthma Exacerbation

Scenario: A 22-year-old patient presents with wheezing, shortness of breath, a respiratory rate of 32 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 88%.

EMS Answer

The first responder should identify wheezing, rapid breathing, low oxygen saturation, and increased work of breathing as signs of respiratory distress. Asthma is mainly a ventilation problem caused by bronchoconstriction, airway inflammation, and mucus production. Immediate first aid includes oxygen and bronchodilator support if available. Oxygen is indicated because the low oxygen saturation shows that the patient is not oxygenating adequately.

MITS Answer

Imaging may include a chest X-ray, although it is often normal in asthma because the main problem is functional airway narrowing rather than structural lung destruction. Sometimes hyperinflation may be seen. Other investigations may include peak expiratory flow or spirometry if the patient is stable enough to perform them.

Radiograph example: Asthma imaging overview

Nursing Answer

Nursing care includes monitoring respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, effort of breathing, and response to treatment. The patient should be positioned upright to improve lung expansion. Medication administration, reassurance, and observing for worsening fatigue are also important because an apparently fast-breathing patient can deteriorate if the respiratory muscles tire.

Case 2: Pneumonia

Scenario: A 65-year-old patient presents with fever, productive cough, and an oxygen saturation of 90%.

EMS Answer

EMS should identify fever, low oxygen saturation, shortness of breath, and possible chest discomfort or productive cough as signs of respiratory compromise. Immediate care includes oxygen, monitoring vital signs, and arranging prompt transfer. The major physiological problem is impaired gas exchange, because infected alveoli are filling with inflammatory material.

MITS Answer

The main investigation is chest radiography. A chest X-ray may show consolidation, which appears as a more opaque region in the lung where air should normally be present. This helps confirm that the alveoli are filled with fluid or inflammatory material and supports the clinical diagnosis of pneumonia.

Radiograph example: Pneumonia radiograph examples

Nursing Answer

Nursing care focuses on oxygenation, positioning, medication support, and monitoring. Because gas exchange is impaired, the patient may need oxygen therapy and upright positioning to improve ventilation. Antibiotics, temperature monitoring, sputum observation, and encouraging deep breathing where appropriate are important parts of recovery and aftercare.

Case 3: COPD Exacerbation

Scenario: A 70-year-old smoker presents with chronic shortness of breath, fatigue, and evidence of carbon dioxide retention.

EMS Answer

EMS should recognise chronic dyspnoea, increased effort of breathing, and carbon dioxide retention as significant warning signs. Oxygen should often be given in a controlled way, because some COPD patients rely more on low oxygen levels as part of their respiratory drive. The immediate priority is to improve breathing while avoiding worsening hypercapnia.

MITS Answer

MITS may support diagnosis with chest X-ray findings such as hyperinflation. Spirometry and arterial blood gases are also important and may show airflow limitation and raised carbon dioxide levels. Imaging helps show structural change, while blood gases help show the functional consequences.

Radiograph example: COPD radiograph examples

Nursing Answer

Nursing care includes monitoring oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, effort of breathing, blood gases, and response to treatment. Breathing techniques, medication support, and energy conservation teaching may be helpful. Because COPD is chronic, aftercare often includes education on inhaler use, smoking cessation, and recognition of worsening symptoms.

Case 4: Pulmonary Embolism

Scenario: A patient presents with sudden shortness of breath, sharp chest pain, tachycardia, and reduced oxygen saturation.

EMS Answer

This is an emergency because pulmonary embolism can rapidly reduce perfusion to part of the lungs and severely impair oxygen delivery. EMS should provide oxygen, recognise the seriousness of sudden dyspnoea with chest pain, and ensure urgent transfer. The main physiological problem is perfusion failure, not simply ventilation failure.

MITS Answer

CT pulmonary angiography is a key imaging investigation because it can demonstrate the clot in the pulmonary vessels. A chest X-ray may be normal or only show non-specific changes, because the alveoli may still be ventilated even though blood flow is blocked. This illustrates why a normal CXR does not exclude pulmonary embolism.

Radiology resource: Pulmonary embolism imaging examples

Nursing Answer

Nursing care includes monitoring oxygenation, pulse, blood pressure, pain, and signs of deterioration. Anticoagulant therapy is often required, and the patient must be observed for complications such as worsening respiratory compromise or haemodynamic instability. Nursing also supports patient education about clot prevention and ongoing treatment.

Case 5: Pleural Effusion

Scenario: A patient presents with shortness of breath, reduced chest expansion on one side, dull percussion, and decreased breath sounds.

EMS Answer

EMS should identify reduced chest expansion, shortness of breath, dull percussion, and decreased breath sounds as clues that fluid may be present around the lung. Oxygen and upright positioning are useful first measures because they can help improve breathing comfort while the patient is transferred for further assessment.

MITS Answer

Chest radiography and ultrasound are both important. A chest X-ray may show blunting of the costophrenic angle or a visible fluid level. Ultrasound is especially helpful because it confirms the presence of pleural fluid and can help guide drainage procedures.

Radiograph example: Pleural effusion imaging examples

Nursing Answer

Nursing care includes monitoring respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, chest pain or discomfort, and supporting preparation for drainage if needed. Aftercare may include observing the patient following thoracentesis, monitoring for recurrence, and helping manage the underlying cause of the fluid build-up.

Case 6: Respiratory Acidosis

Scenario: A patient presents with a reduced respiratory rate, raised carbon dioxide level, and low blood pH.

EMS Answer

EMS should see reduced respiratory rate, possible drowsiness, and signs of poor ventilation as urgent concerns. The immediate problem is inadequate ventilation, which allows carbon dioxide to accumulate. Airway support and assisted ventilation may be needed to prevent further deterioration.

MITS Answer

Arterial blood gas analysis is the main investigation because it directly confirms raised carbon dioxide and low pH. Imaging is not always the first priority unless a structural cause is suspected. In some cases, chest radiography or other imaging may be requested to look for the underlying reason for hypoventilation.

Radiology reference: Respiratory failure imaging overview

Nursing Answer

Nursing care includes close monitoring of respiratory rate, effort of breathing, oxygen saturation, blood gases, and level of consciousness. The main goal of treatment is to improve ventilation so that carbon dioxide can be removed. Nursing support also includes helping treat the underlying cause and watching for further acid–base deterioration.
